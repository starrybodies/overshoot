Notes for grown-ups

How we got 6 liters

This site was built in one session with an AI coding assistant. Nobody has an exact meter on that, so we took a careful published estimate and then leaned hard toward the thirsty side.

Climate scientist Zeke Hausfather logged his own AI coding sessions for eight weeks and estimated that a median session uses about 0.6 kWh of electricity, with a range of 0.25 to 1.2 kWh. We used the top of that range.

Water per kWh has two parts. Data centers evaporate water to stay cool, about 1 liter per kWh in Google's fleet and less on average across the US. Power plants evaporate water too, about 3 liters per kWh on the average US grid (the figure used in the "Making AI Less Thirsty" paper). We rounded the total up to 5 liters per kWh.

1.2 kWh × 5 L per kWh = 6 liters (about 1.6 gallons).
The low end is closer to 0.25 kWh × 1 L per kWh, which is about one cup.

Not counted: the water used to train the AI model, to make the chips and build the data center, or to run the screen you're reading on. To be fair, the footprints below for jeans, phones and burgers do count their whole supply chain.

Two kinds of water

Each page is stamped Tap water or Water footprint. Tap water is water piped in and used directly: flushes, showers, sprinklers. A water footprint counts every liter it took to grow and make something, including rain that fell on the fields. Rain on a wheat field isn't taken from anyone's river, so footprints and tap water aren't perfectly comparable.

The point holds anyway. Counting only tap water, one five-minute shower used more than seven websites' worth.
