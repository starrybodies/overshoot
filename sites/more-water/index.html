The short answer, for grown-ups in a hurry

  • Making this website with an AI helper used about 16 liters of water (4 gallons), counting the data center, the power plant and a share of the AI's training. At the most pessimistic, under 40 liters.
  • That's less than one slice of bread (40 liters to grow the wheat), one five-minute shower (45 liters) or one person's toilet flushes for a day (49 liters).
  • One typical AI question uses about 1.7 mL, around thirty drops, all counted. Google measured 0.26 mL at the data center alone.
  • The water for one hamburger (2,400 liters) would cover about 1.4 million typical AI questions.
  • It still matters in total: US data centers evaporated about 66 billion liters on site in 2023, plus about 800 billion liters at their power plants. How much that matters depends on where they're built and how they're cooled.

This is Glub.

On the night of the rain, Glub could not sleep.

Glub is a small wild thing who keeps track of water: where it goes, and how much. And that night Glub had a question too big for one small head. How much water did it take to make this book?

1

About one big bucket.

This book is a website. A person and an AI helper made it together, talking back and forth.

The AI does its thinking in a great humming building called a data center. Thinking makes heat, and water carries the heat away. The power plants that make the electricity drink water too.

Glub even counted a fair share of the water it took to teach the AI in the first place. Then Glub rounded up, to be fair.

1 big bucket

Even counting the AI's schooling.

About 16 liters (4 gallons), rounded up to one 5-gallon bucket. Our most pessimistic count is under 40 liters. The math is in the notes at the back.

Glub sat down in the wet grass and thought for a long time.

Then Glub asked the smallest questions of all.

One question is about thirty drops.

Google measured its own AI. A typical question used about five drops of water at the data center. The power plant adds about fifteen more, and a fair share of the AI's training adds about thirteen.

A sprinkler spills thirty-odd drops in the blink of an eye. The water in one hamburger could answer a question every minute, all day and all night, for nearly three years.

30 drops

About a third of a teaspoon.

For a typical, short question. Long, complicated requests use more.

19

And what about teaching the AI?

Before an AI can answer anything, it has to learn. Learning takes enormous rooms of computers humming for months, and that drinks a great deal of water all at once.

One AI company, Mistral, measured its big model. Learning, plus a year and a half of answering everyone's questions, used about 281 million liters. That's about what one average American golf course drinks in two years.

But that water is shared by everyone who ever uses the model: millions of people asking billions of questions. This book's fair share is already in Glub's bucket.

281 million liters

Shared by everyone, a thin slice each.

Mistral Large 2, as of January 2025. Training was by far the biggest part of that water.

20

Soon, the AI might live at home.

Some AI models are open: anyone can download one and run it on their own computer. There's no data center and no cooling tower.

Then the only water is at the power plant that makes your electricity. If your power comes from the sun or the wind, there's almost none at all.

Small open models already answer everyday questions on laptops and phones. The kind that could build a whole book like this one still needs a big, powerful computer, but they keep catching up. Models small enough for one home computer have been reaching what the best cloud AI could do within about six months to a year.

And a model only has to learn once. The more people share it, the thinner each slice of its training water gets.

0 cooling towers

Just you, your computer, and the sun.

A mid-sized open model on a home graphics card uses about 2.4 joules per word-piece, so a typical answer takes about a third of a watt-hour. That is close to what a data center uses, but the only water is at the power plant: about 1 mL on the average US grid, and almost none on solar or wind.

21

So is AI's water nothing at all?

No. Glub is a careful wild thing, and careful wild things know that lots of small drops make a river.

People ask AI billions of questions, and more every year. In 2023, data centers in the United States used about 66 billion liters of water to keep cool, and the power plants that ran them used an estimated 800 billion liters more. That water matters most in dry places, so it's fair to ask where new data centers are built and how they are cooled.

American golf courses, for comparison, watered their grass with about 2 trillion liters in 2024.

Keep an eye on the river.

Just don't blame the cup.

  • Take the short shower.
  • Eat fewer burgers.
  • Wear your jeans for years and years.
  • Keep asking good questions, about AI and about everything else.

Notes for grown-ups

Questions people ask about AI and water

How much water does one AI question use?

Roughly a third of a milliliter to two milliliters for a typical short text question, depending on what you count. Google measured 0.26 mL (about five drops) for a median Gemini text prompt in May 2025, counting only water evaporated at its data centers. Adding the water evaporated at power plants (about 3 liters per kWh on the average US grid) brings it to about 1 mL, and a fair share of the model's training to about 1.7 mL, roughly a third of a teaspoon. Long, complex, image or video requests use more.

Why do estimates of AI water use disagree so much?

Because they count different things, for different models, in different places. Some count only the cooling water evaporated at the data center; others add the water evaporated at power plants, a share of training, or the whole supply chain of the hardware. Older models on older hardware also used more per answer, and the same request uses more water in a hot, dry place than in a cool one.

How much water does it take to train an AI model?

A lot at once, then it is shared. Mistral's life-cycle analysis of Mistral Large 2 found that training it and running it for 18 months used about 281 million liters (281,000 cubic meters), mostly for training. The "Making AI Less Thirsty" paper estimated that training GPT-3 used about 5.4 million liters including power plants, about 700,000 liters of it evaporated on site. The makers of today's largest models have not published comparable figures. Google reported that about two-fifths of its machine-learning energy goes to training and three-fifths to answering.

How much water do US data centers use?

About 66 billion liters (17 billion gallons) evaporated on site for cooling in 2023, up from about 21 billion liters in 2014, according to Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, plus an estimated 800 billion liters (211 billion gallons) at the power plants that supplied their electricity. That covers all data centers, AI and everything else. For scale, US golf courses applied about 2 trillion liters of water to their turf in 2024.

Where does AI's water actually go?

Mostly into the air as vapor. Many data centers cool their servers with evaporative cooling towers, and most of the water they lose evaporates; little of it is polluted. Water that is drawn in and later returned is counted as withdrawal; water that evaporates is counted as consumption. Air cooling and closed-loop liquid cooling use far less water on site but more electricity, which moves some of the water use to the power plant. Wind and solar power use almost no water.

Is AI's water use a problem?

Per question it is tiny, but totals are large, growing fast, and local. Water is local: a data center drawing from a stressed aquifer in a dry region matters far more than the same amount in a wet one. One widely cited projection estimated that global AI could withdraw 4.2 to 6.6 billion cubic meters of water in 2027, which its authors compare to four to six times Denmark's annual withdrawal. The useful questions are where a data center is built, how it is cooled, and where its electricity comes from.

Does running AI on my own computer save water?

It skips the data center's cooling water, but the power plant still uses some. An open model on a home computer needs no data-center cooling water. A mid-sized model on a home graphics card uses about 0.3 watt-hours for a typical answer, similar to a data center, so the remaining water is at the power plant: about 1 mL per answer on the average US grid and almost none on solar or wind. Epoch AI finds that models small enough for a single consumer graphics card have reached frontier benchmark scores within about 6 to 12 months.

How does AI's water use compare with everyday things?

At about 1.7 mL per typical question, the water footprint of one hamburger (2,400 liters) equals about 1.4 million questions, and one five-minute shower (45 liters) about 26,000. A cotton T-shirt takes about 2,700 liters, a pair of jeans about 7,500 liters, and one day of watering an average US golf course about 393,000 liters.

How much water did it take to make this website with AI?

About 16 liters (4 gallons), counting the data center, the power plant and a share of the AI's training: roughly 2.4 kWh of electricity for the whole conversation, times about 4 liters per kWh, times 5/3 for training. Our most pessimistic estimate is under 40 liters, which is still less than one five-minute shower.

How we got one big bucket

This book was made in one long conversation with an AI coding assistant: a first draft and several rounds of redesign, about sixteen messages from the person in all. Nobody can meter that exactly, so we built the estimate from published measurements and leaned toward the thirsty side.

Start with electricity. Climate scientist Zeke Hausfather logged eight weeks of his own AI coding. It came to about 150 watt-hours for each message he typed, counting all the work the AI did in reply, with an uncertainty range of roughly 60 to 290. Sixteen messages at 150 Wh is about 2.4 kWh.

Each kWh then costs water in two places. The data center evaporates some water to stay cool: Google's own measurement works out to about 1 liter per kWh, and the US average is lower. The power plant evaporates more: about 3 liters per kWh on the average US grid, the figure used in the "Making AI Less Thirsty" paper. That makes about 4 liters per kWh.

Then there's training. Before it answers anything, a model is trained, and that water counts too. Google reported that over three years about two-fifths of its machine-learning energy went to training and three-fifths to answering. So each kWh of answering carries about two-thirds of a kWh of training with it, and we multiply by 5/3.

2.4 kWh × 4 liters per kWh × 5/3 ≈ 16 liters (4.2 gallons), rounded up to one 5-gallon bucket (19 liters).
Most pessimistic: 4.7 kWh × 5 liters per kWh × 5/3 ≈ 39 liters. Every thing in this book took more than that.

Not counted: water used to make the chips, build the data center, or run the screen you're reading on. The footprints for jeans, phones and burgers do count their whole supply chains, so keep that in mind when comparing.

Tap water and footprints

Some pages count water straight from the tap: flushes, showers, sprinklers. Others count a water footprint, every liter it took to grow and make something, including rain that fell on the fields. Rain on a wheat field isn't taken from anyone's river, so the two kinds aren't perfectly comparable. Each page's small print says which kind it is.

The answer doesn't change if you only count tap water. One person's flushes for a single day, or one five-minute shower, beat even the most pessimistic count for the book.

One question to an AI

Google measured a median text prompt to its Gemini apps in May 2025 at 0.24 Wh and 0.26 mL of water, about five drops. That counts the data center, not the power plant. Adding about 3 liters per kWh for the power plant gives roughly 0.7 mL more, and the 5/3 training share brings the total to about 1.7 mL, or thirty-odd drops. Long or complicated requests use more, and other companies' models differ. Mistral's own analysis put a 400-token answer from its assistant at 45 mL, counting more of the supply chain, so treat these as rough.

2,400 liters ÷ 1.7 mL ≈ 1.4 million questions. At one a minute, that's about 2.7 years.

Training, and models at home

Mistral's life-cycle analysis of Mistral Large 2 found that training it and running it for 18 months used about 281,000 cubic meters of water (281 million liters), with training the largest part. The "Making AI Less Thirsty" paper estimated that training GPT-3 used about 5.4 million liters, counting the power plants. Frontier models today are larger, and their makers haven't published comparable numbers.

Open models run on home hardware skip the data center's cooling water entirely. A 2026 benchmark on a home graphics card (RTX 4060 Ti) measured about 2.4 joules per token for 7-billion-parameter models, measuring the graphics card alone, and about a tenth of that for the smallest models. A 500-token answer from a mid-sized model is about 0.33 Wh, similar to Google's data-center figure, and about 1 mL of power-plant water on the average US grid. Home computers don't batch work the way data centers do, so local AI doesn't automatically save energy. What it changes is where the water goes, and clean electricity can take it to nearly zero.

How soon? Epoch AI finds that open models have recently trailed the best closed models by only a few months, and that models small enough for a single consumer graphics card have reached the frontier's benchmark scores within about 6 to 12 months.
